Why you can trust it

Claude is helpful. Your books need to be right.

Most AI tools will happily fill a gap with a guess. Saybooks is built so that can't happen to your records.

It can't make things up

An amount, a date, a client's name — if Claude doesn't have it, it asks you. It is not allowed to guess, and the system refuses if it tries.

It gets told no

Every rule your books have applies to Claude exactly as it applies to you. When Claude tries something the rules don't allow, it's refused — in a plain sentence, with the reason.

Everything is on the record

Every change says who made it — you or Claude — and why. Refusals are recorded too. You can read the whole history, export all of it, or delete everything.

Here's a real one. Claude tried to confirm an order for a customer who was over their credit limit, and got this back:

Credit check failed for Contoso Ltd. Order $2500.00; available $1000.00 (limit $1000.00 less open AR $0.00 and committed $0.00). Short by $1500.00.

Claude relayed it word for word and asked a person what to do — it didn't split the order or quietly raise the limit. That's the behaviour the rules are written to produce.

For builders

Under the hood: one definition, both surfaces

Every business act is declared once. From that one declaration Saybooks derives the tool Claude sees (over MCP), the form a person sees, the rule that can refuse either, and the audit entry — so the two surfaces can't drift; a contract test fails the build if they do. Each area is governed by a written spec with executable scenarios, and modules are held to a 16-gate contributor contract.

  • Five modules today: order-to-cash, CRM, jobhunt, solo invoicing, and the core — every one spec-first, with replayable conformance scenarios.
  • Open source under AGPL-3.0. Run it, fork it, write a competing module; scenario files speak in acts, so competing implementations run the same tests.
  • Refusals are one sentence, written once in the rule, shown identically to the button and the agent.
